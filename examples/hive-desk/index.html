The quiet arithmetic of municipal water

Six counties, one aquifer, and a spreadsheet nobody had opened since 2019. What the numbers actually say about the next decade.

CB CHRIS BUZAID · 12 MIN READ DRAFT

The pumping station on Route 9 has a sign that has not been repainted since the Clinton administration. Inside, a technician named Ruth keeps a paper logbook beside a terminal that reports the same numbers, and she trusts the logbook.

For eleven years the two records agreed. Then, in the spring of 2019, they stopped — quietly, by about four percent, in a direction that nobody had budgeted for.

“The meter isn't wrong. The meter has never been wrong. What changed is what we decided the meter was measuring.”

That distinction — between an instrument and its interpretation — turns out to be the whole story. The county's allocation formula was written when the aquifer was assumed to recharge on a seasonal cycle.

What follows is an account of how six municipalities arrived at incompatible answers from a single shared dataset, and what it will cost to reconcile them.
